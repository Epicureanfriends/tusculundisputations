## XIII

This section parallels what Velleius says in "On The Nature of the Gods" as to the Epicurean view, but the Epicurean view of the gods is based on proplepsis, which refers to a prompting of Nature but not an idea or opinion.  When Cicero refers to "Consent of all nations" the implication is that there is "consent" which implies an "opinion."  Cicero seems to be saying that Nature gives all men this opinion but it is wrong.  Epicurus says that prolepsis leads ultimately to formation of opinions, but that error occurs in improper conclusions made about the prolepsis, not that the prolepsis itself is wrong. 

This difference in perspective can be viewed as illustrating how Epicurus differs from Cicero (Academy and Stoics) on the standard for right thinking. Cicero would place blame for the error closer to Nature (in 'custom'), and Cicero then points to Reason (logic? syllogisms? geometry?) as what is needed beyond Nature to correct Nature.  Epicurus would place blame for the error on the thinking of men, and Epicurus then points to Nature herself as providing the standard for proper reasoning through the senses, anticipations, and feelings. Nothing beyond Nature - neither logic, syllogisms, or geometry - is needed.


## XIV

Here we see Cicero argue that we should look to "perfect specifimens" for Nature's guidance, not to "the young of all species" as Epicurus points to (as we see Torquatus say in On Ends Book One).

Cicero is arguing that great mean provide for the future, but in response to this we can point out that Epicurus provided for the future of his school and for the children of Metrodorus, but he did not believe in any life after death.  Likewise Diogenes of Oinoanda erected his wall and specifically mentioned future generations, even though he had no thought of his consciousness surviving his death.

## XV

This is just simply false.  No persuasion of immortality is neceessary to act to protect your family and your country. And in fact you can get great pleasure now about thinking of how your actions will impact the future, not only directly but in how you will be remembered by others.  The same motivations are implicit and even explicit in the motivations that Epicurus says we have to pursue friendship.

In sum, Cicero's just making a false argument.  We have plenty of incentive while we are alive to act to benefit those who will come after us, and we don't have to imagine ourselves as ghosts hanging around to observe those results.

## XVI

"But as we are led by nature to think there are gods, and as we discover, by reason, of what description they are," << this part is fairly consistent with Velleius and prolepsis, but again, the "consent of all nations" that follows next goes too far.

As for souls going underground Cicero again seems to admit that this is preposterous and suited mainly for actors trying to impress women and children such as in a theatre.  Again Cicero says "at present it seems to be removed" indicating that the error was no longer popular.

Note this slam at the sensations: "To withdraw the mind from sensual objects, and abstract our thoughts from what we are accustomed to, is an attribute of great genius"

And this confirms the popularity of Pythagorus:  "His disciple Pythagoras greatly confirmed this opinion, who came into Italy in the reign of Tarquin the Proud: and all that country which is called Great Greece was occupied by his school, and he himself was held in high honour, and had the greatest authority: and the Pythagorean sect was for many ages after in such great credit, that all learning was believed to be confined to that name."

## XVII

The ancients gave no reasons for their opinions except "through numbers or definitions!"

Another interesting reference to the relationship between Plato and Pythagorus, and to how so many would prefer to be wrong with Plato than right with the anti-Platonists.

Again proof by mathematics:  "Do we, then, doubt, as we do in other cases, (though I think here is very little room for doubt in this case, for the mathematicians prove the facts to us,) ...."

- Earth in Center of Universe;
- Souls as lighter bodies mount upwards after death.
- Soul as a NUMBER (which he admits is not clear)


## XVIII

Cicero criticizes Dicaemarchus as never feeling grief and so incapable of perceiving that he had a soul, and criticizes Aristoxenus for suggesting that there is a parallel with music and that the soul is a harmony.  Cicero says to leave those speculations to Aristotle.

And Cicero says that he will have nothing at all to do with atomism. Was Democritus saying that the atoms are warm and can breathe and are alive?

Cicero agrees that the soul is like air or fire, another commonality with the Stoics.

## XIX

CIcero sides with what sounds like a Stoic-like position that souls go up into the air because they are light and warm, but Cicero seems to think the souls stay there.

This is where Cicero actually argues that it will be good to be dead because we will after death be in a better position to pursue the kind of knowledge that we longed for when alive.

## XX

According to Cicero the things we see when we are dead will be much more impressive than what we see when we are alive.

This is a very important section where Cicero is showing how the leading schools were representing the senses and the body as holding us back.  Cicero says it is the soul itself which sees and hears

"when we shall be nothing but soul, then nothing will interfere to prevent our seeing everything in its real substance, and in its true character."

## XXI

This too sounds like criticism of Epicurean views for giving thanks to Nature and to Epicurus - reverencing him as a god!  And he criticizes the view that Epicureans are delivering us from "Fear" when no one believes the stories of the underworld.

Cicero says that it is unseemly to boast about this, and it makes them look stupid to assert that they might have believed the tales of the gods if not for Epicurus.

Cicero "says nothing either way" as to whether the soul survives death -- but he does say that Plato had lots of reasons and seems to have convinced himself!

## XXII

More criticism of Epicureanism for finding it inconceivable that the soul could live apart from the body.  After all, it's not like we understand the soul when it IS in the body, any more than when it is outside the body!  (Note - this is why Epicurus wants a theory on what the soul is!)

Cicero says he himself thinks that the soul does not belong in the body.

This points out why we need a theory of epistemology, such as referenced in Philosdemus "On Methods of Inference." -- "For unless we are to say that we cannot apprehend the character or nature of anything which we have never seen, we certainly may be able to form some notion of God, and of the divine soul when released from the body."

Note that Epicurus does not say that there is no such thing as a soul, as Dicmarchus and Aristoxenus.

## XXIII

This eternal motion argument is a word game of defining something as eternal because it is eternally moving and neither gives motion to anything else nor is itself moved by anything else.

This is a first cause / argument from causation discussion.

The argument is that whatever moves itself is eternal, and thus the soul must be eternal because the soul can move itself (?)

What about the souls of animals?  Is this why they don't think animals have souls?  "For this is the peculiar nature and power of the soul; and if the soul be the only thing in the whole world which has the power of self-motion, then certainly it never had a beginning, and therefore it is eternal.”

This is another slam against Epicurus and all who dissent from Plato/Socrates


## XXIV

Cicero doesn't even want to explain the origin of these divine properties, because if he could do that then he might be able to explain how they might cease to exist.

Cicero says pleasure and pain are shared with beass, but we also have memory

The Meno parodox of living from a past life.

Plato says that if a thing is not eternal it does not really exist!


## XXV

Here we have Cicero arguing that if he were to take a firm position about anything (which as a skeptifc he won't) it would be that the soul is divine.  He says it's unimaginable that this wondrous thing like memory could be associated with the earth (dirt) or this gloomy atmosphere.  Cicero also says that it's absurd to think of the soul in analogy to a vessel (Epicurus?), or in analogy to a writing table (Aristotle?).  He implies that Pythagorus said that the highest "pitch" of wisdom is that of naming things (the language question).

Cicero is using the amazing abilities of memory to imply that these abilities mean memory is divine - more than bodily.

This is of people and actions that have benefited humanity hearks back to Lucretius saying that Epicurus' achievements were greater than those of Hercules.

And the comparison of Archimedes as having done something analogous to Plato's god in Timeaus to setting in motion the stars and planets.  That also has an echo in Lucretius when Lucretius asks how the gods could have created the universe without a pattern.


## XXVI

Cicero says poetry is evidence of divine nature, but philosophy is especially so.  Philosophy is parent of all the arts and a gift of the gods.

Memory is also a divine invention.

The soul is divine; it is in fact a god, and it doesn't really matter if it is air or fire or Aristotle's fifth nature - it is a god.  *But it can't be air or water as that is beneath divinity.)


## XXVII

The soul must have been created by god, and therefore it must be eternal.  God must be disting from all mortal concretion, "acquainted with everything," and "giving motion to eveyrthing - itself endued with perpetual motion."

## XXVIII

NOTE:  No need to read this section in full.  It's basically:  Look at the world and contemplate what you see and you must acknowledge that there is a designer of it.

Just like the eye can't see itself, the soul cannot apparently see itself, but it's not necessary that it have that ability.

Thus, though you see not the soul of man, as you see not the Deity, yet, as by the contemplation of his works you are led to acknowledge a God, so you must own the divine power of the soul, from its remembering things, from its invention, from the quickness of its motion, and from all the beauty of virtue.

## XXIX

Cicero says the soul is seated in the head, but you need not be certain of that any more than you are certain where God lives.

Cicero points out that Socrates died voluntarily, saying "a few days before, when he might have been easily freed from his confinement, he refused to be so, and when he had almost actually hold of that deadly cup, he spoke with the air of a man not forced to die, but ascending into heaven."
